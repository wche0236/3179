Australian Recreation Insights

Outdoor Recreation in Australia

Australians are most active in everyday, informal ways
and in shared public places. This dashboard explores
who takes part, how habits have changed, where
recreation happens and what holds people back.

01

Participation and people

Who Participates, and How?

Start with the scale of participation, then see how it
differs across activities, genders, life stages and states.

Popularity

Walking reaches 11.4 million adults, nearly three times bush walking.

Recreational walking is the most popular activity shown, ahead of bush walking (3.9 million), running or jogging (3.7 million) and swimming (3.4 million). The gap suggests that accessible, everyday activities reach far more people than specialised ones. The data also reveals that most Australian recreation is ordinary.

Gender gap

Golf and fishing are about four-fifths male, while walking leans female.

Men make up 81.3% of golf participants and 77.9% of fishing participants. Women make up 58.4% of walking participants, and kayaking is almost evenly split. These are shares of participants, not participation rates, so they show who takes part rather than why.

Life stage

With age, activity shifts from sport towards informal activity.

Among active teenagers (15–17), 60% do both sport and non-sport activity and 26% do sport only. Among active adults aged 65+, 64% do non-sport activity only and just 8% do sport only. Participation changes form rather than simply falling, though the chart shows active people only.

Place

Weekly participation ranges from 67% to 78.3% across states.

The Australian Capital Territory has the highest rate (78.3%). Differences of up to 11.3 percentage points show that a national average can hide regional variation. The data cannot explain these differences, so they are a starting point for looking at local opportunities and needs.

02

Change over time

How Have Recreation Habits Changed?

Having seen who takes part, look at how participation
in five popular activities has moved since 2017.

Long-term change

Bush walking grew fastest of the five activities.

Between 2017 and 2023, bush walking grew by 75%, compared with about 9% for walking. Everyday walking stayed far larger in total numbers, but bush walking gained significant ground over the period.

Recent trends

Cycling fell while bush walking surged.

Cycling peaked at 3.2 million participants in 2021 and fell 12% by 2023. Meanwhile, bush walking surged past both swimming and cycling, suggesting a strong shift toward nature-based recreation.

03

Places and public spaces

Where Does Recreation Happen?

Public spaces are the most common setting for activity,so
look at national parks as one example of shared space.

Settings

Public spaces are the most common setting for activity.

Public spaces account for 31 of every 100 settings where active adults take part, ahead of clubs and community facilities (24), gyms and commercial venues (23) and home (15). Public space is a broad category, not limited to national parks, but it shows how much recreation depends on shared, open places.

Park locations

Mapped national parks cluster along the east coast.

Of the 280 national parks mapped, Queensland has 70 and New South Wales 58, together about 45%. Meanwhile, the vast inland regions remain largely devoid of parks in this dataset, highlighting an uneven geographic footprint.

Park visits

Three coastal parks account for 43% of NSW national park visits.

NSW national parks recorded 65.5 million visits in 2024–25, including 28 million in Greater Sydney. Blue Mountains National Park (7.8 million), Royal National Park(4.9 million) and Ku-ring-gai Chase National Park (4.3 million) lead the parks shown. This suggests that proximity to major population centres and accessible coastlines is the primary driver of park visitation.

04

Motivations and access

What Encourages or Prevents Participation?

Shared places are open to everyone, but not everyone takes part.
Look at what motivates people, what holds non-participants back,
and what taking part costs.

Motivations

Motivations shift from fun towards health with age.

Fun and enjoyment are strongest among 15–17-year-olds, while physical health or fitness is strongest at 65+. Hobbies and learning a new skill fade with age, and being outdoors or enjoying nature stays important in every age group. This suggests recreation messages could differ by life stage.

Barriers

Women report cost and dislike of activity more often than men.

Among non-participants, 8.4% of women cite cost, against 4.5% of men, and 16.8% of women dislike physical activity, against 11.5% of men. Men more often cite a physical job. Poor health or injury is the top reason for both, so support may need to differ by group.

Cost

Women's spending falls 40% after its peak, while men's stays high.

Women's average spending peaks near $1,760 at ages 45–54 and falls to about $1,055 at 65+. Men's peaks near $1,790 at 55–64 and stays around $1,715 at 65+. Women also cite cost as a barrier almost twice as often as men, but these data cannot show whether the two are connected.

In summary

What We Can Learn

Recreation is part of everyday life in Australia,
but participation varies across ages and activities.
Accessible public spaces and support for different needs
can help more people enjoy recreational opportunities.
